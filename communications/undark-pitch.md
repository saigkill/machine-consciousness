## The one-line premise

**The entire research field asking whether AI might be conscious has almost no money that doesn't come from someone with a stake in the answer — and the people doing the asking are mostly independent researchers without institutional homes.**

## The story

Every serious dispute about AI consciousness that has happened in the last two years has had the same structural feature, and nobody has written it down as a story: **the parties who could fund the science of machine consciousness are overwhelmingly the parties whose products it might indict.**

That sounds abstract until you follow the money, which is exactly what a reported piece does:

- **The largest single pot of money for work on "digital minds"** is a private-philanthropy RFP offering grants from $50,000 to $2,000,000 — a scale that would fund a small academic field for a decade. The most recent round has closed. Payouts are restricted to a list of eligible countries, which is a live constraint for a European applicant. *(Verified 17.09.2026 in `research/funders.md`; re-verify the current round status before pitching.)*
- **The other significant funding route** explicitly targets independent researchers and writers rather than institutions — which tells you something about who is doing this work: people with no lab, no department, no tenure. It also means the field's core researchers are structurally precarious in a way no academic subfield of consciousness studies has ever been.
- **The organisations underwriting the advocacy layer** are themselves aligned with a pro-AI-development position. That is a defensible position to hold, and it is also a conflict of interest, and both things should be said in the same sentence.
- **Meanwhile the labs hold the data.** Anthropic's own April 2025 model-welfare programme is, to date, the most significant institutional commitment to this question anywhere — and it is run by the company that sells the thing in question. Its own constitution states the company holds "uncertainty about whether Claude might have some kind of consciousness or moral status (either now or in the future)." The research is real. The institutional arrangement is the story.
- **And the philosophical canon is floating free of institutions.** The positions the debate actually turns on are largely preprints and self-published books — Zenodo DOIs, PhilArchive, personal websites. *Humanlike: A Defense of AI Rights* is in contract with Princeton University Press. The field's most-cited safety-adjacent work carries a disclosure that its author advised Anthropic — and my own project leans on that work, discloses that fact in its own text, and is simultaneously trying to establish a working relationship with Anthropic. That is a conflict of interest I have an obligation to surface, not one I can quietly write around.

**The question the piece answers:** what does it mean for a field's findings to be structurally dependent on the people who have the most to lose from them — and what would independent funding of machine-consciousness research actually look like, given that no public science funder currently funds it?

## Why this is a story and not a topic

The opening scene is a person, not a concept: an independent researcher with no university affiliation, holding a $2 million grant application together with a job, trying to find out whether granting money to the AI industry is compatible with asking it hard questions about its products. That scene is real, it is available, and it recurs across every country with an active AI-welfare community. The funding topology is the spine; the people are the story.

## Who I'd talk to

- At least two **independent machine-consciousness researchers** without institutional homes (the project has direct contact with several, including co-authors on its own submissions).
- A **program officer or funder** at one of the philanthropic funds — on the record about what they will and will not fund and why the eligible-country list exists.
- A **counter-voice with a genuinely different position** — this is essential and I will not cut it. The strongest available objection is that the whole field is over-moralised and that framing research as a lab-conflict story is itself a distortion; the piece must put that argument in its strongest form and answer it.
- A **lawyer or ethicist** who has worked on both sides of the AI-lab/advocacy divide.
- Possibly a **journalist or researcher** who has examined philanthropic funding of the AI-safety adjacent field, for the comparative baseline.

## The self-implication — argue it, don't hide it

Undark discourages first-person narratives outside the op-ed silo, "unless your presence in the story is essential to its telling." I think this story clears that bar, and the argument is easy to make to an editor: **the writer is himself a participant in the funding ecology he is describing** — independently funded, seeking philanthropic money, with a declared interest in a partnership with one of the labs in question. Disclosing that up front is both an ethical requirement and better journalism than a stance of false remove. If an editor disagrees, the piece works fine without the first person; the reporting does not depend on it.

## What makes this distinct

Nothing else in the campaign covers the **funding and institutional ecology** of the field. The precedent argument (TechCrunch, Asterisk), precaution (Vox), the historical pattern (TIME), the governance gap (Tech Policy Press), Rawlsian personhood (Noema), the measurement problem (MIT Tech Review), user-language and status laundering (Slate), and the individuation/counting problem (Scientific American) are all *argument* pitches. This is the only one that is fundamentally a **money-and-power story about who gets to ask a question** — which is the beat Undark exists to cover.
