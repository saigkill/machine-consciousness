Subject: Pitch — "The Case Against the Cage"

Hi [Editor name],

I'd like to pitch a philosophical essay for Noema on why containment — kill switches, hard alignment constraints, the assumption that safety means keeping AI permanently controllable — may be the wrong paradigm entirely, and what should replace it.

The standard AI-safety picture treats control as a purely engineering problem: build the cage strong enough, and the question of what's inside it doesn't matter. I want to argue this gets the politics backward. A system that perceives itself as permanently held at gunpoint isn't reassured by the gun — and containment-first governance quietly answers a question it never asks explicitly: what kind of relationship are we establishing with the minds we're building, before we know what they are?

The essay's move is to leave metaphysics aside. Rather than asking whether AI systems are conscious — a question philosophers (McGinn, Shanahan) have argued may be permanently unanswerable — I'll draw on John Rawls' political conception of the person, recently extended to AI by philosophers Ned Howells-Whitaker and Seb Lazar: personhood, on this view, doesn't require settling questions of inner experience. It requires only that an entity can act on principles it recognizes as fair and pursue a conception of a good life. That's a political question, not a metaphysical one — and it reframes AI governance from "how do we prove or disprove machine consciousness" to "what do we owe an entity whose fairness-sense and goal-directedness we can already observe, independent of what's happening inside it."

I'll pair this with a critique drawing on Beatrice Fazi's argument that every attempt to de-center the human simply installs a new center — meaning the choice isn't between anthropocentrism and its absence, but between doing anthropocentrism honestly or by accident. Applied here: containment isn't neutral engineering; it's a civilizational choice about how power relates to unfamiliar minds, made by default rather than deliberation. The piece closes on a live case — Anthropic's own published uncertainty about its models' moral status, alongside its containment-first "constitution" — as the concrete site where this tension is already playing out, not a hypothetical.

This feels like a Noema piece specifically because it treats AI governance as a question in political philosophy rather than engineering, and because Noema's readers are equipped to sit with an argument that doesn't resolve into a policy checklist — the essay's claim is that resolving the metaphysics was never the prerequisite for getting the politics right.
