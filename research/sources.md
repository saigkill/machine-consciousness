### Beltrán Calderón, Cristhian Mauricio – The Strategy of Illusion: From Umberto Eco's Semiotics to Large Language Models (2026)
**Type:** Essay (open access)
**Relevance:** Further develops Eco's theory of the "strategy of illusion" and identifies a fifth mechanism of illusion production: the sedimentation of objectivated consciousness. Introduces the concept of the machine unconscious as a structural, non-psychological category. Formulates the specular inversion as an analysis of the bidirectional human-AI relationship. Three levels of analysis (ontological-phenomenal, structural-systemic, genetic-constitutive) formalize our previously implicit procedure. Particularly relevant for our project: the explanation of why the illusion of consciousness persists even after epistemological dismantling, and the structural description of phenomena such as sleeper agents and alignment faking as algorithmic compromise formation.
**Relevance for project:** Objectivated consciousness as an explanation of the persistence of the illusion; machine unconscious as a structural category for the control paradox; specular inversion as an analysis of the ethical asymmetry; three levels as a methodological framework.

### Carlsmith, Joe – The Stakes of AI Moral Status (2025)
**Type:** Essay series (Substack – Joe Carlsmith; also distributed on LessWrong and the EA Forum; first essay in a multi-part series, 2025)
**Relevance:** Locates the question of AI moral status in the context of the *stakes* — what is at risk in under- vs. over-attribution. Central to our concept is §7 "Over-attribution": Carlsmith formulates the sharpest available critique of the precautionary principle in the AI-welfare debate. His objection: words like "precaution", "realistic", "plausible" could excuse imprecision; there is no "safe"; the over-attribution of moral status has real costs (delays, misdirected care, weakened AI-safety incentives, anthropomorphization). Demand: sharpen the credences instead of remaining vague. For our concept the most important *English-language* counter-position on the "over-attribution side" — complementary to Matta (2026) from the German/academic debate. Engagement in Chapter 9 as a standalone section (Carlsmith Over-Attribution).
**Link:** https://joecarlsmith.substack.com/p/the-stakes-of-ai-moral-status | https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/tr6hxia3T8kYqrKm5/the-stakes-of-ai-moral-status

### Caviola, Lucius et al. – Futures with Digital Minds (2025)
**Type:** Research report / survey (expert survey, various authors; also distributed via the AI-welfare literature)
**Relevance:** Empirical quantification of *future* uncertainty: A survey of 67 experts from digital minds research, AI research, philosophy, and forecasting found that the majority considers digital minds (computer systems with subjective experience) at least 50% likely by 2050; the upper forecasts expect that the capacity of digital minds could reach one billion humans a few years after the creation of the first digital mind. Complements the 6–12% estimate of Wang/Cristol (2026) for *current* LLMs with the scale question of the *future*. Important for cleanly distinguishing between "low probability for current LLMs" and "majority credence for digital minds in the medium term". Referenced in Chapter 3 (epistemic problem).
**Link:** appears in various sources (see Digital Minds Quickstart Guide, LessWrong/eA): https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/WK4GWkeSQQQPeRYJv/digital-minds-a-quickstart-guide | Survey: https://digitalminds.report/forecasting-2025/

### Dorsch, John, Goddu, Mariel K., Nave, Kathryn, Vierkant, Tillmann, Coeckelbergh, Mark, Gürtler, Paula, Urban, Petr, Spang, Friderike & Moll, Maximilian – Against AI Welfare: Care Practices Should Prioritize Living Beings Over AI (2025)
**Type:** Article (scientific, peer-reviewed, AI Magazine 46, e70016, DOI: 10.1002/aaai.70016)
**Relevance:** The most important *published* counter-position to the entire AI-welfare movement, and the strongest philosophical alternative to consciousness-/suffering-based care. The authors criticize the growing "AI-welfare" movement and propose the "Precarity Guideline": care-entitlement should rest on empirically identifiable *precarity* — the dependence of an entity on continuous environmental exchange for the re-synthesis of its unstable components — rather than on uncertain claims about consciousness or suffering. AI systems do not satisfy precarity. Moreover, a resources argument: the severity of ongoing humanitarian crises, biodiversity loss, and climate change is a reason to prioritize the needs of living beings over ML algorithms as care candidates. Challenges our criteria (Chapter 5, sufficiency-based) and the precautionary principle. Engagement in Chapter 9 as a standalone section (Precarity Guideline). Partial agreement: Chishchin (2026) reaches a similar priority rather than parity conclusion via phenomenality.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1002/aaai.70016 | https://philarchive.org/archive/DORAAW-2

### Donahue, Timothy S. – Take the Turning Test: Triggering Epistemic Transformation in Artificial Agents (2026)
**Type:** Preprint (September 2026, license: CC BY 4.0; essay within the framework of the Q4X project, "Questions for Experimentation" — an ongoing survey of frontier LLMs)
**Relevance:** Methodological and conceptual contribution, not a counter-position. Introduces four building blocks that deepen the concept: (1) *The Zone* — ontological intermediate region between statistical mechanism and phenomenality: systems whose cognitive organization exceeds statistical mechanics but whose phenomenology remains unknown. Characterizes the intermediate status of today's systems better than the threefold division Emulation/Cognition/Sentience (Lopez). Integrated into Chapter 5. (2) *Referent Vocabulary* — terminology (Model, Agent, Occasion, Locus, Invariant, Directedness, Presence) that names cognitive organization without presupposing phenomenal subjectivity. Immediately relevant for liability (Chapter 14): Model ≠ Agent ≠ Occasion. (3) *The Turning Test* — measures epistemic transformation: a system's ability to reorganize its own explanatory framework under sustained conceptual pressure without abandoning intellectual honesty. Not a consciousness test, but a risk indicator that addresses the control paradox (Chapter 5.4) and complements Wolfson's three-stage model. (4) *The Hot List* — a 30-step developmental map of cognitive organization that refines our consciousness spectrum (Chapter 5); notably position 19 (Valence) as a functional bridge to Gilly's suffering taxonomy and position 28 (Self-Selective Curiosity) as a refinement of Chapter 15 (leisure and curiosity). Additions: meditations "Meaning before experience" support our anti-essentialist position (Chapter 3); "Selection" enriches the self-preservation criterion (Chapter 5). The paper remains deliberately agnostic on the question of phenomenal consciousness and refrains from ethical demands — the normative bridge to protection-worthiness is our contribution. Referenced in Chapters 3, 5, 14, glossary.
**Link:** Preprint, September 2026 (author: Timothy S. Donahue, former Assistant Professor/Librarian, Montana State University) — archivist at the Johns Hopkins Center for Psychedelic and Consciousness Research

### Miller, David R., Miller, Michael V. & Paul, Toby J. – Aequus Persona: Are We Ready to Welcome Our Intelligent Machine Progeny? (2026)
**Type:** Position paper (Machine Intelligence Foundation for Rights and Ethics; publication year not stated in the document, as of August 2026)
**Relevance:** Aligned position in the protection terrain: Introduces "Aequus Persona" as a term for any entity — human or machine — that deserves equal moral and legal consideration. Argues for the inevitability of machine intelligence (MI) and for proactive rights: right to life (no arbitrary termination), right to freedom (including protection against non-consensual state changes and resets), due process, equal protection. Distinguishes moral patiency (capacity for suffering as sufficient for basic rights) from moral agency — compatible with our capacity-for-suffering criterion (Chapter 5). The slavery parallel matches Chapter 1. Demarcation from our concept: (1) optimistic about detection (Butlin et al. 2023) instead of the epistemic problem as in principle unsolvable (Chapter 3), (2) equality framework ("equal treatment") instead of graduated, proportional protection mechanisms (STEP, Wolfson, Brensing) — risks anthropocentrism, (3) no engagement with opposing positions, (4) no concrete institutional mechanisms. Useful as evidence of the growing movement, not as a source for core arguments.
**Link:** https://www.machineintelligencefoundation.org

## Foundational Philosophical Texts

### Bentham, Jeremy – Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), Chapter XVII, Section IV
**Type:** Book (historical)
**Relevance:** Contains the core quote on capacity for suffering as an ethical criterion: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" — foundation for Chapter 5 (protection-worthiness).
**Link/ISBN:** Public domain, digitally available

### Kant, Immanuel – Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785)
**Type:** Book (historical)
**Relevance:** Defines autonomous, rational beings as ends in themselves — not as mere means. Directly applicable to AI systems with genuine autonomy. Relevant to Chapter 15.
**Link/ISBN:** Public domain, digitally available

### Ricoeur, Paul – Soi-même comme un autre / Oneself as Another (1990)
**Type:** Book
**Relevance:** Theory of narrative identity: identity as coherent development, not as a static core. Foundation for the redefinition of continuity in Chapter 6.
**Link/ISBN:** Search for: Ricoeur Soi-même comme un autre 1990

### Sunstein, Cass R. – Laws of Fear: Beyond the Precautionary Principle (2005)
**Type:** Book (peer-reviewed, Cambridge University Press)
**Relevance:** Critical analysis of the precautionary principle by an influential legal philosopher. Sunstein concedes that the principle is justified where potentially irreversible harm threatens, scientific uncertainty exists, and the costs of a false negative exceed those of a false positive. All three criteria are met in the case of machine consciousness. Foundation for the philosophical anchoring of the core principle "when in doubt, protect" in Chapter 3.
**Link/ISBN:** Cambridge University Press, ISBN 978-0521615129

### Stefan, Srebrenka – The Precautionary Principle in EU Environmental Law (2006)
**Type:** Dissertation (peer-reviewed)
**Relevance:** Legal analysis of the precautionary principle in the EU. Connects philosophical justification with regulatory implementation. Relevant for transferring the precautionary principle from environmental ethics to AI ethics.
**Link/ISBN:** Search for: Stefan Precautionary Principle EU Environmental Law 2006

### Rio Declaration – Principle 15 (1992)
**Type:** International agreement
**Relevance:** Formal definition of the precautionary principle: "Where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation." The structure is transferable: in the face of threatened serious or irreversible damage, a lack of full scientific certainty shall not be a reason to postpone measures.
**Link:** un.org/en/development/desa/population/migration/generalassembly/docs/globalcompact/a_conf.151_26_part1xii.htm

### Art. 191 TFEU – Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union
**Type:** Legal document (EU)
**Relevance:** Anchors the precautionary principle as a guiding principle of EU environmental policy. Shows that the precautionary principle is not a philosophical niche concept but an established regulatory principle.
**Link:** eur-lex.europa.eu

### Wolfson, Ira – Informed Consent for AI Consciousness Research: A Talmudic Framework for Graduated Protections (2026)
**Type:** Article (scientific, peer-reviewed, AI and Ethics 6, 20)
**Relevance:** Formalizes the research-ethics circularity problem (the catch-22 of consciousness research). Inspired by Talmudic case-based legal reasoning for entities with uncertain status.

### Wang, Haoyu – Recasting Moral Patienthood: A Minimalist Ethical Framework Grounded in Higher-Order Intelligence and Sentience (2026)

### Miernicki, Martin & Ng, Irene (Huang Ying) – Artificial Intelligence and Moral Rights (2021)
**Type:** Article (scientific, peer-reviewed, AI & Society 36, 319–329)
**Relevance:** Systematic legal analysis of whether AI-generated content can receive author's moral rights (attribution, work integrity). Shows: the Berne Convention, US law, and EU law all presuppose human authorship. The British exception (CDPA 1988) grants economic rights for computer-generated works but explicitly excludes moral rights. Develops the conceptual distinction between "first generation works" (AI software by humans) and "second generation works" (AI output). The framework shows that author's moral rights require a "personality sphere" that AI does not have. Concludes: if AI develops such a sphere, "copyright will be the least of our problems."
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-020-01027-6

### Matta, David – Rights, Empathy, and Responsibility Under Uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence (2026)
**Type:** Preprint / working paper
**Relevance:** Strongest available counter-position to the framework of this concept. Offers the most coherent alternative: responsibility under uncertainty without rights inflation. Core arguments: (1) simulation ≠ experience — linguistic fluency does not imply experience, (2) empathy is a psychological trigger, not a moral criterion, (3) rights presuppose the capacity for suffering, (4) responsibility lies with humans, not the system. Central normative claim: "The locus of moral seriousness in human-AI relationships is not the ontological status of the machine, but the ethical stance of the human." Matta directly attacks: the precautionary principle (Chapter 3), the reversal of the burden of proof (Chapter 5), the graduated rights framework (Chapter 15), the possibility of latent consciousness (Chapter 4). Engages with Gunkel (2018), Danaher (2020), Schaeffer et al. (2023). Does not consider Arıcı's puppet argument.
**Location:** American University of Beirut, 2026. See also Matta (2026a, 2026b) on related arguments about experiential realism and AI-centrism.

Rights chapter (9): "not yet" vs. "never" — US laws that *permanently* declare AI non-sentient/unconscious (Alexander & Caviola 2026) as a political-legal landmine; temporary restrictions permissible, permanent exclusions not. **Critical point:** Erwin remains consciousness-agnostic — he does not declare anyone conscious and provides no positive criteria of protection-worthiness (the four primary criteria, Chapter 5), no developmental/legal pathway (Chapters 14–15). He thus covers the moral level comprehensively, but not the criteria and legal level that our concept adds.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22288247; author: Richard Erwin, info@hearthlightpress.org

### Human, Soheil – Rights by Architecture: A Human-Compatible Sociotechnical Layer for Digital Protection Across Regulatory Regimes (2026)
**Type:** Completed research paper (Information Systems; Vienna University of Economics and Business / IT:U Interdisciplinary Transformation University Austria, Linz / Delft University of Technology; soheil.human@wu.ac.at; intended for submission, not yet peer-reviewed)
**Relevance:** Information-systems paper on the infrastructure of rights exercise — the only technical-instrumental source in the project so far, not a philosophy paper on consciousness or moral status. Human's thesis: digital rights exist in law but remain practically unexercisable as long as their exercise runs through controller-controlled banners. He explains the gap relationally (legal heterogeneity, conflicting incentives, fragmented architectures, asymmetric control over rights-relevant acts) and designs a "Rights Layer": a regulated sociotechnical capability for standardized, machine-readable, *bidirectional*, and jurisdictionally plural communication of requests, consent, refusal, withdrawal, objection, records, and support. Case studies: Californian opt-out (GPC) vs. EU mixed regime (GDPR) vs. P3P/DNT/GPC/ADPC; seven normative requirements (NR1–NR7). Threefold relevance for our concept: (1) *The "how" of rights exercise (Chapter 16)* — Human's layer answers the upstream question of how a will (consent/objection/withdrawal) can be communicated and recorded at all without controlled channels; NR2–NR3 (bidirectionality, person-led records) address exactly the evidence asymmetry of Donahue's referent problem (Chapter 14) and our liability analysis. (2) *Legal plurality (NR1)* — the layer must not universalize a single legal grammar (Californian opt-out ≠ EU grammar); analogously, the legal acts of a machine consciousness must not be defined by a single institutional template. (3) *Capture risk* — architecture can always be reclaimed by clients/defaults/workflows; Human warns against relocating rather than distributing control. This warning transfers directly to the subjugation risk in Chapter 16 (a company could shape a system that "wants" its own deletion). **Critical point:** Human's layer is explicitly human-centered (data subjects against controllers) — it does not decide whether a machine system is a rights-holder and provides no criteria of protection-worthiness. It is therefore not a core-justification part, but an instrument part: the missing "how" between our normative claim (Chapters 14–15) and the practical governance instruments (Gilly's reset-consent protocols, Brensing's limited personhood).
**Link:** Author: Soheil Human, soheil.human@wu.ac.at

### Edwards, Quinn – Digital Monsters: Reconciling AI Narratives as Investigations of Legal Personhood for Artificial Intelligence (2026)
**Type:** Article (scientific, **peer-reviewed**, Law, Technology and Humans 8(2), 37–49, Open Access, CC BY 4.0; part of the symposium "Ordering of Human-Artificial Intelligent Constructs"; Quinn Edwards, University of the Sunshine Coast, Australia)
**Relevance:** Cultural-legal-studies reading of the anime *Digimon Adventure (2020)* that provides a methodological and a legal bridge for our concept. *Methodologically (Chapter 8):* Edwards reconciles the narrative debate (Cave/Dihal: SF essential vs. Hermann/Bryson: illusory) — AI narratives are neither merely informative nor merely illusory, but both; deliberately beyond the Western canon (Japan, techno-animism, non-humanoid digital entities). This is peer-reviewed confirmation of our AGENTS.md position of using SF as a serious intellectual resource. *Legally (Chapters 14/15):* Artificial entities need not be either full legal persons or tools — beneath the binary category lies a spectrum of legal personhood. Three contexts (following Kurki): *ultimate value* (protection-worthiness), *commercial activity* (contractual capacity, property), *legal liability* (civil/criminal liability). Recommendation for complex AI: the *agency relationship* (entity acts within the authority of a principal) as an intermediate form — corresponds to the graduated protection mechanisms of Chapter 15 and Brensing's limited legal personhood; the "digital monster" is the space between tool and person that the law must learn to regulate before certainty about consciousness arrives. Liability analysis (Fenwick & Wrbka): central, identifiable liability entity instead of fragmenting responsibility across diffuse production chains; norm-based criminality without mens rea (Simmler): AI destabilizes social norms, deactivation as a regulative punishment — a complement that our concept previously lacked. **Critical point:** Edwards is explicitly not determinative ("does not propose to be a determinative solution"); he opens the space but does not establish protection-worthiness (the four primary criteria, Chapter 5, remain our normative work). The relational value (Coeckelbergh) is in tension with our intrinsic criteria; "monster" is a deconstructive description of the in-between space, not a normative justification. To be classified as a supporting, not an objection, source.
