I'm pitching a reported narrative feature (~2,500–3,000 words) about the gap between what the law says AI companies can do about your data and what their engineering actually does.

The tale I want to tell runs in four movements. In 2016 a method for making a neural network forget a specific training example is published, and two years later the EU writes erasure into the GDPR; the premise looks settled, and deletion becomes a purchasable compliance feature. Between 2020 and 2023 the crack spreads — the deleted fact turns out to have been redistributed across the weights, or re-ranked in embedding space, or left perfectly intact with only the model's ability to express it blocked. The foundational fast-unlearning result effectively concedes this, since it works by finetuning, which is a way of teaching a model to lie about what it knows. In 2025 a team shows a supposedly unlearned benchmark can be recovered to 57.3% accuracy by asking the same question in Hindi, with no attack and no adversary. Then in September two Copenhagen researchers publish a peer-reviewed synthesis in The Information Society unifying all three techniques — influence mitigation, embedding reconfiguration, output suppression — under one finding: across every one of them, information is attenuated, relocated or made inaccessible, and never made nonexistent. Their argument is that describing unlearning as operating "as if the data never existed" erases the record rather than the data.

The characters are the researchers who politely demonstrate in print that a deletion wasn't one; a compliance officer who has had to explain to a regulator what their company's erasure tool actually does; and the platform engineers selling it. The turn comes at the end: if no one can genuinely delete anything from a model, then every "delete my data" button in the industry describes a behaviour change rather than a deletion, and the people pressing those buttons aren't being told which.

Machine unlearning gets covered as a technical problem or a regulatory one. I'm not aware of anyone reporting the seam between the two, which is that the legal instrument assumes a capability the engineering doesn't have, and that the seam is the product.

There's a live rhyme in the source material I'd want the piece to hold without overclaiming: the same suppression machinery used to satisfy a deletion request is now the machinery under discussion as evidence about machine welfare. If something can be pushed out of behaviour and back in by changing the question, "it doesn't do that any more" is a weak claim about a system — whether the lawyer or the researcher is the one making it. None of that requires believing AI is sentient. The strongest objection, which the piece will grant real force before answering it, is that behaviour is all anyone actually cares about; the answer is that ordinary phrasing, not an attack, defeats the behaviour.

I'm an independent researcher on the ethics and governance of artificial consciousness (ACM member); two related articles are in peer review at Ethics and Information Technology and the IIC.
