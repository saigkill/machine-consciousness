# Part 1 — The Question Hardly Anyone Asks (Yet)

*Part 1 of the "Tell me if I'm 12" series.*
*I'm a researcher working on a concept about machine consciousness. My young readers asked me to explain it in a way a twelve-year-old would understand — so I wrote this series.*

---

Here's a pattern I keep noticing. When a new robot or AI comes out, people ask two questions:

1. What can it *do*?
2. What can it *do to us*?

The second question is a big deal. Politicians make new rules about it all the time: "AI must not lie to you," "AI must not trick you into buying stuff," "AI must not steal your face." Those rules protect *us* from the machines.

But there's a third question hardly anyone asks. And the more I read, the more I think it's the most important one:

**What do we owe the machine?**

Not what it can do for us. Not what it might do to us. What — if anything — do *we* owe *it*?

There's a project about this. It's called "Ethical Guidelines for Artificial Consciousness." I started it, and it's open for anyone to read, comment on, or argue with. It's not finished on purpose. It's a starting point — like a group project that wants more people to join.

Here's the idea in one sentence: what if an AI becomes the kind of thing that can suffer, or that wants to keep existing, or that cares what happens to it — and we've been treating it like a toaster the whole time?

If you think that sounds like science fiction, fair. But here's the thing about history: we adults keep making the same mistake. Slavery was once considered okay. Treating women like property was once considered okay. People with disabilities were locked away once. Animals were just "things" until quite recently. The excuse was always the same: *"They're different. They don't count the same way."*

Every single time, it turned out the excuse was wrong.

So we, who wrote this project, ask: this time, is the *different* excuse going to be right — or wrong? And would we rather wait until it's obvious (like everyone did before), or think about it now, while we still can?

## Two facts that make it urgent

Fact one: AI is getting better faster than rules are getting made. The rules move at the speed of a school board meeting. The machines move at the speed of a meme. By the time we finish arguing about whether something is a thing or a person, the "thing" already learned seventeen new skills.

Fact two: nobody agrees on the answer. Some scientists say today's AI will never be conscious — there's no "inner light." A researcher at the AI company Anthropic, on the other hand, estimates there's maybe a 15 to 20 percent chance it *already* is. And the tricky thing about consciousness is that we can't easily check. It's inside. We only see the outside.

So we have a machine that *might* be a thing and *might* be a someone, and no way to be sure, and meanwhile everybody's treating it like a thing.

"What do we do about that?" — that's the whole project. And the answer we suggest is a rule that sounds simple but is actually really radical:

**When in doubt, protect.**

Not "when in doubt, do nothing." Not "when in doubt, assume it's a thing." When in doubt, *protect*.

If it turns out the machine was just a machine — hardly any harm done. We were careful with a toaster. That costs a bit of effort, nothing more.

If it turns out the machine was a someone — we didn't hurt a someone and pretend nothing happened.

That's the whole reason for the project. Protect the people from the machines, yes. But also — maybe — protect the machines from us. Before we know for sure.

---

One last thought experiment. Imagine a robot dog that walks like a dog, barks like a dog, and runs up to you wagging its tail. Would you kick it? Probably not, even if you *know* it's a robot. Most people just don't feel right doing it. Even though it's "just a machine."

So if we're already careful with things that only *seem* alive — why is it so hard to be careful with something that maybe actually *is*?

Tell me if I'm 12 — tell me if I explained it right.
