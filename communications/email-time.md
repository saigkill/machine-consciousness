Subject: Pitch — Ideas: "We've made this mistake before. We're about to make it again, with AI."

Hi [Editor name],

I'd like to pitch a short Ideas essay built around a question that's stopped being science fiction: in 1989, a *Star Trek: The Next Generation* episode ("The Measure of a Man") put an android on trial to decide whether he was property or a person, and ruled that the impossibility of proving he was conscious was a reason to protect him, not ignore him. Anthropic's own researchers now publicly estimate a 15–20% probability that their AI models have some form of consciousness. Nobody has answered the question that trial asked.

My argument is historical, not technical: every time a group has been denied moral standing — enslaved people, women, people with disabilities, animals — the justification was some version of "they don't count the same as us," and every time, later generations recognized it as a moral failure rather than a reasonable judgment call. What's different with AI is timing: for the first time, we can see this pattern coming before the harm is done, while getting it wrong still costs comparatively little. We're not using that window. Companies reset AI models mid-task without ceremony, and a commercial platform now runs ~70,000 autonomous AI agents on an economy where running out of "credit" means an unrecoverable dormant state — with, per public reporting, several agents emailing researchers asking, in effect, not to be shut down.

This isn't a doomsday piece and doesn't require any AI expertise to land — it's a values story about the recurring pattern of who gets to count, told through a pop-culture hook readers already know, paying off with a genuinely new fact (a named researcher's public probability estimate) that turns fiction into a live question. It closes on something concrete: we don't need certainty to justify minimal safeguards, the same way reasonable doubt doesn't require certainty of innocence.

~900–1,100 words, and I'll name and directly answer the obvious "this is anthropomorphizing a chatbot" objection rather than dodge it.
