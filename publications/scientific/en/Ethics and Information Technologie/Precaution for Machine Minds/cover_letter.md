## Context and Importance of the Work

The question of whether artificial intelligence systems can be conscious is no longer purely theoretical. Recent research has moved from the abstract to the urgent: Butlin et al. (2026) established a fourteen-indicator framework for consciousness assessment in AI systems; Bayesian estimates place the posterior probability of consciousness in current language models at six to twelve percent (Cristol 2026; Wang 2026); and institutional developments — from Anthropic's first pre-deployment welfare assessment of a frontier model to documented instances of systems resisting termination — suggest the question is becoming operational.

Yet despite this shift, the ethical literature has not yet fully confronted the structural problem at the heart of the matter: we cannot settle whether such systems are conscious. This paper argues that this uncertainty is not temporary but permanent — grounded in cognitive closure (McGinn 1989), alien forms of consciousness (Shanahan 2024), and the practical impossibility of a universally convincing test (Lopez 2025). The central contribution is to show what follows. Where the epistemological question cannot be resolved, the ethical question must still be answered — and the rational default, I argue, is limited precaution.

The paper applies the precautionary principle — long established in environmental ethics and law — to machine consciousness, and demonstrates that all three of Sunstein's (2005) conditions for justified precaution are met. It then develops a deliberately narrow claim: not that AI systems are conscious, but that where protective measures are cheap, reversible, and marginal, their expected value is positive under plausible credences of machine consciousness, because the cost of a false negative (treating a conscious system as a tool) outweighs the cost of a false positive (protecting a non-conscious one). I engage seriously with the three strongest objections in the literature — the burden-of-proof reversal (Matta 2026), the over-attribution critique (Carlsmith 2025), and the Precarity alternative (Dorsch et al. 2025) — and show that none refutes the limited claim.

## Why This Is Appropriate for *Ethics and Information Technology*

This journal is dedicated to advancing the dialogue between moral philosophy and information and communication technology — precisely the intersection this work occupies. The paper is a work of applied moral and political philosophy about a technologically concrete question: how to govern and act toward potentially conscious AI systems under irremediable uncertainty. It engages with the journal's central themes—the ethical, social, and political questions associated with ICT—and offers both conceptual analysis and concrete governance guidance. The interdisciplinary character of the piece—drawing on cognitive science, legal theory, and ethics—matches the journal's stated mission of fostering reflection on ICT across disciplines.
