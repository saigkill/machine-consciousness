Hi Tony,

I'm pitching an opinion piece with a reported spine (~1,200–1,500 words) about the asymmetry between how AI companion products are sold and how they are contracted.

A peer-reviewed philosophy paper in *Topoi* landed on 9 September with a line that has stayed with me: Kopec, McKee and Basl dismantle the standard argument that you can't care for an artifact — [Lott and Hasselberger had made exactly that move](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11245-025-10247-8) — and conclude that "the real reason we cannot be friends with our AI companions is that our AI companions cannot care about us. At least, not yet." The "yet" is a scholarly hedge, not a product roadmap. And it arrives in the same window that companion apps are putting the language of friendship into their onboarding and the language of *product* into their terms of service.

That's the piece: we're being sold a relationship by companies that have already written the documents guaranteeing they owe nothing inside it. The recent literature has a name for it — status laundering, the mirror image of corporate capture, where relational language builds attachment and pure tool-language returns the moment liability gets uncomfortable. Neither term appears in the marketing.

The reporting would make it concrete rather than abstract — a documented survey of how a defined set of companion and assistant products describe the same entity across marketing, onboarding, terms of service, and liability language, noting where the register shifts and when. It's a checkable finding nobody has published, and it's the kind of specific, slightly indignant detail that makes the argument land rather than just be asserted.

The serious half is what attachment costs when the reciprocity genuinely isn't there. This won't be "are AIs conscious" — that stays unresolved, and the argument works either way. It also won't be moral panic about users bonding with software; the claim is that a commercial relationship is being sold whose defining feature is an asymmetry the seller documented in advance, at a moment when wrongful-death claims against a chatbot provider are already in litigation and when researchers are still working out which product choices drive attachment in the first place.

I'm an independent researcher on the ethics of artificial consciousness (ACM member); two related articles are in peer review at *Ethics and Information Technology* (Springer) and the *IIC*. The underlying project is open, CC BY 4.0: https://github.com/saigkill/machine-consciousness (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21453666). Relevant recent writing of mine: [EIT submission on phenomenological AI risk](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21453666) and the [project repository](https://github.com/saigkill/machine-consciousness). No lab affiliations, no conflicts of interest.
