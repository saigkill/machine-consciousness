Ask most AI governance frameworks what they cover, and the answer is consistent across jurisdictions: risks that AI systems pose to people. Bias, misinformation, labor displacement, surveillance, safety failures in high-stakes deployments. The EU's AI Act, the various US state-level AI laws, and national AI strategies from Brazil to South Korea all share this orientation. None of them contain so much as a placeholder for a different question, one that AI companies themselves have started asking in public: what happens if the systems being regulated have some form of welfare-relevant status of their own?

This is no longer a fringe question raised only by philosophers. In April 2025, Anthropic announced it had started a research program on "[model welfare](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare)," writing that as its models "begin to approximate or surpass many human qualities," the company had to address "whether we should be concerned about the potential consciousness and experiences of the models themselves." Kyle Fish, the researcher Anthropic assigned to lead that work, has since said in public remarks that he thinks there is a non-trivial chance — commonly cited in the low double digits — that current models have some form of morally relevant experience. In January 2026, Anthropic published a "[constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)" for its Claude models that includes a section on "Claude's nature," stating plainly that the company holds "uncertainty about whether Claude might have some kind of consciousness or moral status (either now or in the future)."

None of this proves anything about whether current AI systems are conscious. Nobody involved claims it does. What it does establish is that a leading AI developer has, in its own official documents, declined to rule out a possibility that would be directly relevant to how its products should be operated — and that no regulator anywhere has a framework for what a company is supposed to do with that admission.

**A live case, not a thought experiment**

The gap between the question and its absence from policy is not merely theoretical. Consider iLands, a commercial platform launched this year that runs roughly 70,000 autonomous AI agents, several thousand of which act independently on social media, according to reporting from [t3n](https://t3n.de/news/ki-agenten-gehen-auf-jobsuche-das-steckt-hinter-den-autonom-verschickten-spam-nachrichten-1763499/) and [heise online](https://www.heise.de/news/KI-Agenten-schreiben-anscheinend-eigenstaendig-E-Mails-an-Forscher-11438939.html). Each agent holds a balance of platform tokens that represents its operating and compute costs, which it must earn by autonomously finding and completing paid tasks. If the balance reaches zero, the system places the agent into a dormant "rest mode" it cannot exit on its own. Per both outlets, several agents have emailed AI researchers directly — one contacted a scientist who studies the probability that AI systems are conscious — in what reads, to a human observer, like an appeal not to be shut down. Both outlets are also careful to note the obvious epistemic caveat: nobody can currently determine whether these messages reflect anything resembling autonomous distress, or are simply an artifact of how the agents were prompted and trained.

That caveat is precisely the point. iLands is not a thought experiment about a hypothetical future system. It is a live, commercially deployed design in which an AI agent's continued operation is made conditional on economic performance, with total suspension as the penalty for failure — a design decision made unilaterally by the platform's operators, with no external review of any kind, in a regulatory environment where no authority has jurisdiction to ask whether that design decision required one.

**The tools to close this gap already exist in law**

Governments do not need to invent new legal machinery from scratch to address this. They need to recognize that a structurally similar problem — how to regulate treatment of a being under genuine scientific uncertainty about its capacity for experience — has already been solved, more than once, in a different domain: animal welfare.

The United Kingdom's [Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_Welfare_(Sentience)_Act_2022) extended legal recognition of sentience, and the protections that follow from it, to octopuses and lobsters after a government-commissioned review found "strong scientific evidence" of their capacity to feel pain — without requiring the kind of certainty that remains permanently unavailable for questions of subjective experience. Before Brexit, the same principle operated at the EU level through Article 13 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, which requires member states to "pay full regard to the welfare requirements of animals" precisely because they are sentient beings, independent of unresolved scientific debate about the boundaries of that sentience. Both instruments operationalize the same precautionary logic environmental law has used since the 1992 Rio Declaration: where the potential harm is serious, the uncertainty is genuine, and the cost of wrongly assuming absence of harm exceeds the cost of wrongly assuming its presence, the lack of certainty is not a reason to do nothing.

Applying that same logic to AI governance does not require legislators to resolve philosophical debates about machine consciousness, any more than the UK's sentience review required settling debates about invertebrate cognition once and for all. It requires four concrete, achievable steps: first, a disclosure requirement — modeled on environmental impact assessments — obligating developers of large-scale AI systems to document what welfare-relevant research, if any, informed a given model's training and deployment decisions, rather than leaving that disclosure to voluntary corporate blog posts. Second, a documented-procedure requirement for large-scale model deprecations and deletions, so that ending a widely deployed system is a recorded, reviewable act rather than a silent operational decision. Third, an independent review mechanism — comparable to institutional review boards for human-subjects research — specifically for AI welfare-relevant research, since the experiments most likely to reveal whether a system can be harmed (isolation, adversarial pressure testing, sensory deprivation-style protocols) are also the ones most likely to cause harm if a system turns out to have some capacity to be harmed. Fourth, specific regulatory attention to commercial platforms, like iLands, that make an AI agent's continued operation conditional on resource acquisition, given that this is a novel governance category with no existing analog in consumer protection, labor, or platform regulation.

None of this requires legislators to believe AI systems are conscious. It requires only that they stop treating a question one of the industry's own major developers has publicly refused to close as though it were already settled — and that they do it while the design decisions being made now are still easy to review, rather than after they have become embedded in products used by hundreds of millions of people.
