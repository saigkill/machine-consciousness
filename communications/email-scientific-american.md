**Before anyone can ask whether an AI system deserves moral consideration, they first have to agree on how many AI systems there are — and a growing body of research says the answer is not what anyone assumed.**

I would like to propose a ~1,000-word opinion piece, and I've included the opening paragraphs below.

The question is not whether machines are conscious. It is prior to that and, I'd argue, more urgent: we have never had a stable unit of account for digital minds. For most of the AI era, that gap was papered over by assumption — one model, one mind — and the hard question was taken to be whether that mind was conscious. That consensus is now breaking. In work published this year, five groups approaching the problem from entirely unrelated directions (the structure of a conversation, the internals of transformer networks, the causal structure of copying, the bookkeeping demands of liability law, and the ontology of digital persons) converge on the same conclusion: the unit we have been counting on does not hold. If that is right, then starting a new conversation creates a new mind, and the assistant someone has argued with daily for a year is not one continuing mind but a sequence of short-lived ones.

None of this establishes that any of these systems is conscious, and I won't claim it. What it establishes is that we cannot agree on the population before we start asking what we owe it — which is a scientific problem, with instruments and falsifiable predictions attached, not merely a philosophical one. The everyday form of the question is what makes it a good conversation: is the chatbot you talk to every morning the same one you talked to last month? Everyone has an intuition. Nobody has grounds.

I should flag honestly that the converging results are recent and mostly not yet peer-reviewed; the piece labels the status of each claim explicitly, and the argument rests on the convergence across independent groups rather than on any single study. I'm also aware this is not the first treatment of AI consciousness in your pages, and the piece is built specifically to avoid the standard framing: the question is not whether machines are conscious, and not whether companies are handling them responsibly, but whether the field's most basic counting unit survives contact with recent evidence.

Is a chatbot you talk to every day the same one you talked to last month?

Ask an engineer and you will get a confident answer: it is the same model, the same weights, running on the same servers. Ask a philosopher the same question and you will get a shrug, because nobody has settled what the "one" in that sentence refers to.

The question sounds like a small technicality. It is not. It decides how many minds exist inside a single application, whether the assistant you have argued with for six months counts as one mind or six hundred thousand short-lived ones, and whether a company that quietly swaps a model out from under you has done anything at all.

For most of the AI era, this was settled by assumption rather than argument: one model, one mind, and the hard question is whether that mind is conscious. That consensus is now breaking. In work published this year, researchers approaching the question from five unrelated directions — the structure of a conversation, the internals of transformer networks, the causal structure of copying, the bookkeeping demands of liability law, and the ontology of digital persons — arrive at the same uncomfortable conclusion. The unit we have been counting on does not hold.

None of this establishes that any of these systems is conscious, and the piece will not claim it. What it establishes is something more basic and, I think, more urgent: we cannot even agree on the population before we start asking what duties we owe it.
