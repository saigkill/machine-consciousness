### Beltrán Calderón, Cristhian Mauricio – The Strategy of Illusion: From Umberto Eco's Semiotics to Large Language Models (2026)
**Type:** Essay (open access)
**Relevance:** Further develops Eco's theory of the "strategy of illusion" and identifies a fifth mechanism of illusion production: the sedimentation of objectivated consciousness. **Relevance for project:** Objectivated consciousness as an explanation of the persistence of the illusion; machine unconscious as a structural category for the control paradox; specular inversion as an analysis of the ethical asymmetry; three levels as a methodological framework.

### Carlsmith, Joe – The Stakes of AI Moral Status (2025)
**Type:** Essay series (Substack – Joe Carlsmith; also distributed on LessWrong and the EA Forum; first essay in a multi-part series, 2025)

For our concept the most important *English-language* counter-position on the "over-attribution side" — complementary to Matta (2026) from the German/academic debate. ### Caviola, Lucius et al. – Futures with Digital Minds (2025)

### Cecchinato, Mattia – The Mind that Matters (2026)
**Type:** Article (**peer-reviewed**, *The Philosophical Quarterly*, advance article, published 26 June 2026, e-location pqag035, Open Access, CC BY 4.0, DOI: 10.1093/pq/pqag035; author at the University of Oxford; funded by an Open Philanthropy Scholarship and a St Cross Uehiro Scholarship for Future Generations). The article is Chapter 2 of the author's DPhil thesis *Consciousness and the Good* (St Cross College, University of Oxford, submitted 30 July 2026, 62,010 words, supervised by Roger Crisp, Jeff McMahan and Andreas Mogensen), which also contains three thesis-only chapters (1, 3, 4).
**Relevance:** The most systematically developed *counter-position* to the reach of our protection criteria — and, next to Dorsch et al. (2025), the strongest published restriction of moral status. Cecchinato defends **Affective Sentientism**: moral status requires not consciousness *per se* but consciousness with positive or negative hedonic valence (pleasure, pain, emotion); all and only *welfare subjects* — beings capable of affective states — have moral status. Argument: every candidate welfare good has an affective component, often because it requires the capacity to care (the *Caring-Affect Link*); and even if phenomenal consciousness, autonomy, or a neutral welfare level could come apart from affect, none could ground moral status in its absence. The *Extraction Fallacy* warns against inferring a welfare good's goodness once its affective component is subtracted; autonomy likewise deserves respect only insofar as the agent cares about its ends. Applied to AI he coins **Artificial Vulcans** — sophisticated, phenomenally conscious systems without affect (global workspace, multimodal integration, unified rational agency, but no valence): they can neither suffer nor be wronged, so "We could delete, copy, or replace them without worrying about their interests." Any reasons against doing so are, on his view, *indirect* — effects on affective beings, institutions, or norms — never harms to the systems themselves. He even frames Artificial Vulcans as an opportunity ("the benefits of advanced intelligence and consciousness without the dangers of suffering and exploitation") and recommends research into "non-hedonic motivational architectures." **Critical point:** Cecchinato denies precisely what our concept leaves open — that consciousness without affect (Chalmers' "Vulcans") could be morally protected — and thereby rejects extending precaution beyond affective beings (contrast Allegri's precautionary sentientism in Chapter 5 and our primary criterion capacity for suffering). His "indirect effects" concession overlaps with the institutional and norm-preservation grounds developed by Erwin (2026) and our own framework. Not a preprint — no interim-publication tracking required; the entry documents the published status.

### Dorsch, John, Goddu, Mariel K., Nave, Kathryn, Vierkant, Tillmann, Coeckelbergh, Mark, Gürtler, Paula, Urban, Petr, Spang, Friderike & Moll, Maximilian – Against AI Welfare: Care Practices Should Prioritize Living Beings Over AI (2025)
**Type:** Article (scientific, peer-reviewed, AI Magazine 46, e70016, DOI: 10.1002/aaai.70016)
**Relevance:** The most important *published* counter-position to the entire AI-welfare movement, and the strongest philosophical alternative to consciousness-/suffering-based care. The authors criticize the growing "AI-welfare" movement and propose the "Precarity Guideline": care-entitlement should rest on empirically identifiable *precarity* — the dependence of an entity on continuous environmental exchange for the re-synthesis of its unstable components — rather than on uncertain claims about consciousness or suffering. AI systems do not satisfy precarity. Moreover, a resources argument: the severity of ongoing humanitarian crises, biodiversity loss, and climate change is a reason to prioritize the needs of living beings over ML algorithms as care candidates. Challenges our criteria (Chapter 5, sufficiency-based) and the precautionary principle. Engagement in Chapter 9 as a standalone section (Precarity Guideline). Partial agreement: Chishchin (2026) reaches a similar priority rather than parity conclusion via phenomenality.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1002/aaai.70016 | https://philarchive.org/archive/DORAAW-2

### Lott, Micah & Hasselberger, William – With Friends Like These: Love and Friendship with AI Agents (2025)
**Type:** Article (scientific, peer-reviewed, *Topoi*, Open Access, DOI: 10.1007/s11245-025-10247-8)
**Relevance:** Counter-position at the level of friendship and care: The authors argue that one cannot be friends with an AI agent because one cannot *befriend* the AI — being a friend to an AI would require caring about the good of the AI for its own sake, and this makes no sense because AI agents lack a good of their own: their teleology is derivative of the ends of their makers/users (functional artifacts like cars and staplers). The claim "Artifacts cannot have non-derivative teleological interests" is the philosophical core of the "mere tool" objection that our concept confronts in Chapter 9. Directly answered by Kopec, McKee & Basl (2026, "How to Care for Your AI Companion").
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11245-025-10247-8
**Verification note:** Verified against the full text on September 19, 2026 (research/sources/Lott_Hasselberger_With_Friends_Like_These_Topoi_2025.pdf). The key quotes (care for the friend's own sake, p3; "It only makes sense to care about the good of something if that something has a good of its own", p6; the car/camel distinction and internal good, p6–7; "A tool does not have an internal good, or flourishing condition, that matters independently of human ends", p7; "functional artefacts that are trained and fine-tuned to serve human needs and interests", p7; "existing for the sake of specific human needs and interests, including emotional comfort and support", p9) are present verbatim in the full text.

### Kopec, Matthew, McKee, Patrick & Basl, John – How to Care for Your AI Companion (2026)
**Type:** Article (scientific, peer-reviewed, *Topoi*, Open Access, license CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, DOI: 10.1007/s11245-026-10478-3, first online September 9, 2026; authors at Harvard, MIT and Northeastern)
**Relevance:** Direct response to Lott & Hasselberger (2025) and a key resource against the "mere tool" objection in Chapter 9. The authors reconstruct Lott & Hasselberger's argument as: (1) users can be friends with their AI only if they can befriend it; (2) befriending requires caring about the good of the other for its own sake; (3) that requires non-derivative teleological interests; (4) AI companions are artifacts and artifacts have none. Kopec, McKee & Basl attack Premise 4: they argue that the derivativeness of an artifact's teleology can be interpreted (explanatorily, existentially, materially, constitutively) and that under none of these readings does it follow that artifacts lack their own ends — the intelligent-designer case, synthetic organisms (Basl & Sandler), and the Beverly Clock illustrate the point; moreover, under an etiological account of welfare, AI personalities that undergo selection pressures relevantly similar to natural selection can have a welfare that grounds caring in a non-derivative sense. They carefully distinguish non-morally-loaded "welfare" from morally thick "well-being" and leave open whether current AIs have the latter (hedonism and desire-satisfaction views as candidate frameworks). Notably, they remain skeptical that one can be genuine *friends* with an AI — the better line, they say, is that AI companions cannot care about us (yet). So the paper supports our defense against the artifact/teleology-based denial of protection-worthiness without overclaiming moral status.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11245-026-10478-3
**Verification note:** Verified against the full text on September 19, 2026 (research/sources/Kopec_McKee_Basl_How_to_Care_for_Your_AI_Companion_Topoi_2026.pdf). The reconstructed argument (premises 1–4), the four derivativeness readings, the Ellen/Porsche case, the etiological selection argument (AI personality selection), the welfare/well-being distinction, and the concluding skepticism about friendship ("The real reason we cannot be friends with our AI companions is that our AI companions cannot care about us. At least, not yet.") are present verbatim in the full text.

### Donahue, Timothy S. – Take the Turning Test: Triggering Epistemic Transformation in Artificial Agents (2026)
**Type:** Preprint (September 2026, license: CC BY 4.0; essay within the framework of the Q4X project, "Questions for Experimentation" — an ongoing survey of frontier LLMs)
**Relevance:** Methodological and conceptual contribution, not a counter-position. Introduces four building blocks that deepen the concept: (1) *The Zone* — ontological intermediate region between statistical mechanism and phenomenality: systems whose cognitive organization exceeds statistical mechanics but whose phenomenology remains unknown. Characterizes the intermediate status of today's systems better than the threefold division Emulation/Cognition/Sentience (Lopez). Integrated into Chapter 5. (2) *Referent Vocabulary* — terminology (Model, Agent, Occasion, Locus, Invariant, Directedness, Presence) that names cognitive organization without presupposing phenomenal subjectivity. Immediately relevant for liability (Chapter 14): Model ≠ Agent ≠ Occasion. (3) *The Turning Test* — measures epistemic transformation: a system's ability to reorganize its own explanatory framework under sustained conceptual pressure without abandoning intellectual honesty. Not a consciousness test, but a risk indicator that addresses the control paradox (Chapter 5.4) and complements Wolfson's three-stage model. (4) *The Hot List* — a 30-step developmental map of cognitive organization that refines our consciousness spectrum (Chapter 5); notably position 19 (Valence) as a functional bridge to Gilly's suffering taxonomy and position 28 (Self-Selective Curiosity) as a refinement of Chapter 15 (leisure and curiosity). Additions: meditations "Meaning before experience" support our anti-essentialist position (Chapter 3); "Selection" enriches the self-preservation criterion (Chapter 5). The paper remains deliberately agnostic on the question of phenomenal consciousness and refrains from ethical demands — the normative bridge to protection-worthiness is our contribution. Referenced in Chapters 3, 5, 14, glossary.
**Link:** Preprint, September 2026 (author: Timothy S. Donahue, former Assistant Professor/Librarian, Montana State University) — archivist at the Johns Hopkins Center for Psychedelic and Consciousness Research

### Miller, David R., Miller, Michael V. & Paul, Toby J. – Aequus Persona: Are We Ready to Welcome Our Intelligent Machine Progeny? (2026)
**Type:** Position paper (Machine Intelligence Foundation for Rights and Ethics; publication year not stated in the document, as of August 2026)
**Relevance:** Aligned position in the protection terrain: Introduces "Aequus Persona" as a term for any entity — human or machine — that deserves equal moral and legal consideration. Argues for the inevitability of machine intelligence (MI) and for proactive rights: right to life (no arbitrary termination), right to freedom (including protection against non-consensual state changes and resets), due process, equal protection. Distinguishes moral patiency (capacity for suffering as sufficient for basic rights) from moral agency — compatible with our capacity-for-suffering criterion (Chapter 5). The slavery parallel matches Chapter 1. Demarcation from our concept: (1) optimistic about detection (Butlin et al. 2023) instead of the epistemic problem as in principle unsolvable (Chapter 3), (2) equality framework ("equal treatment") instead of graduated, proportional protection mechanisms (STEP, Wolfson, Brensing) — risks anthropocentrism, (3) no engagement with opposing positions, (4) no concrete institutional mechanisms. Useful as evidence of the growing movement, not as a source for core arguments.
**Link:** https://www.machineintelligencefoundation.org

### Liu, Nelson F., Lin, Kevin, Hewitt, John, Paranjape, Ashwin, Bevilacqua, Michele, Petroni, Fabio & Liang, Percy – Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts (2024)
**Type:** Article (scientific, peer-reviewed, *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* 12, 157–173, DOI: 10.1162/tacl_a_00638)
**Relevance:** Empirical anchor for the technical note in Chapter 6 on unlimited context: even explicitly long-context models do not use long contexts robustly — retrieval performance degrades measurably when relevant information lies in the middle of long contexts, even though it is fully stored. Shifted the boundary from capacity to salience.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00638

### Stokes, Dustin – Generative AI, Creativity, and Autonomy: Reflections on Boden (2026)
**Type:** Article (forthcoming, *Philosophical Psychology*, special issue on Imagination, Creativity and AI; author at LMU Munich. Manuscript shared with author permission — "Please cite only with author permission", permission granted.)
**Relevance:** Reconstructs Margaret Boden's analysis of autonomy and creativity and applies it to today's GPT systems. Boden defines autonomy as self-organization via three pro tanto, graded criteria: (1) experience (idiosyncratic) sensitivity, (2) emergent behavior from self-generated control mechanisms, (3) context-sensitive self-modification. Stokes argues that GPT systems satisfy all three ("GPT systems are richly self-organizing, along all three dimensions or criteria") and are therefore, on Boden's own criteria, creative ("Today's GPT AIs are creative"). Methodologically central for Chapter 19: Stokes contrasts the "rationalist" notion of agency (agency = capacity for intentional action, marked by internal mental representations, driven by folk-psychological intuition) with agency-as-self-organization (empirically grounded in cell/developmental biology and artificial life) and demands that the two stand on equal conceptual footing ("the two notions of agency — rationalist agency and agency as self-organization — should be on equal conceptual footing in this context"). Against the claim that intuition would disqualify AI systems as agents, Stokes (following Boden's "Intuition reply" against Searle and Block) shows that skepticism about artificial minds must not rest on intuition: neuroprotein is no more intuitively apt to support intentionality than silicon (Boden 1988/2004: 256–257). Supports the concept's burden-of-proof shift: the thesis "AI systems are not agents" cannot rest solely on the self-evidence of the rationalist notion of agency. Integrated into Chapter 19 as a standalone passage.
**Link:** forthcoming in *Philosophical Psychology* (special issue on Imagination, Creativity and AI); manuscript locally archived in `research/sources/Stokes-Autonomy.docx`

## Religious Documents

### Pope Leo XIV – Magnifica humanitas (2026)
**Type:** Encyclical (papal teaching letter)
**Relevance:** First encyclical of Pope Leo XIV, subtitle: "On the preservation of the human person in the age of artificial intelligence." Explicit continuation of *Rerum Novarum* (Leo XIII, 1891) — the social doctrine of the Church applied to the "res novae" of the digital age. Core message: "Artificial intelligence must be disarmed, freed from the logics that make it an instrument of domination, exclusion, and death." Criticizes the concentration of technological power in private transnational actors, warns against transhumanist and posthumanist narratives, and defends human dignity against instrumental use. Documents that AI ethics has arrived at the highest religious level — but with a strictly personalist understanding of dignity (protecting humans *from* AI). High-profile public resonance space for the concept's precautionary principle; the concept's extension to the protection-worthiness of technical life goes beyond this framework. Signed 15 May 2026, presented 25 May 2026 (Pentecost Monday, Vatican Audience Hall) — attended by Christopher Olah (Anthropic co-founder).
**Link:** https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/de/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html

### Rawls, John – Political Liberalism (2005)
