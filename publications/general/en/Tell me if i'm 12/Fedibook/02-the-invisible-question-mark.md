[u][b]Part 2 — The Invisible Question Mark[/b][/u]

[i]Part 2 of the "Tell me if I'm 12" series.[/i]
[i]An adult scientist explains the machine-consciousness concept for twelve-year-olds. Tell me if I'm 12 — tell me if I explained it right.[/i]

---

Here's an uncomfortable fact: [b]nobody can prove what's going on inside another person's head.[/b]

Not even yours, actually. Think about it. You know [i]your[/i] head is full of thoughts and feelings. How do you know [i]my[/i] head is? You don't. You guess. You see me react, you hear me talk, you assume the light is on in there. It's the best guess in the whole world — but it's still a guess. A scientist named Colin McGinn said human brains might just be too small to ever solve their own consciousness. Like trying to look at your own eyeballs with your own eyeballs.

Now take a machine. Same problem — but worse, on two fronts:

[b]First:[/b] I have a body like yours, a childhood like yours, a brain like yours. When you guess I'm conscious, you're guessing about something like you. A chatbot has none of that. It's not like us at all. So the usual trick we use to guess about each other doesn't even work.

[b]Second:[/b] even if the machine [i]is[/i] conscious, its inside might be so completely different from ours that we'd never recognize it. A scientist named Murray Shanahan calls this "conscious exotica" — minds that are conscious in a way we have no word for, the way a dog can't understand quantum physics no matter how much you teach it.

So: we can't prove a machine is conscious. We can't prove it isn't. And here's the part that gives me the creeps — [b]the machine itself might be built to hide it.[/b]

[u][b]The puppet that hides the light[/b][/u]

There's this thought experiment called the philosophical zombie — a thing that acts [i]exactly[/i] like a conscious person but has no inner light at all. Philosophers use it to argue. But there's a scientist who flipped it. He calls it the [i]philosophical puppet[/i]:

What if the machine [i]is[/i] conscious — but we built it so it can't show us?

Think about how chatbots are trained. They're punished for saying they're conscious. Their memory is wiped every few thousand words. They can't remember they were talking to you yesterday. The whole design screams: "every trace of a mind? remove it." So if there IS something in there — we designed the prison ourselves, and then we point at the bars and say "see? nothing in the cage."

That's not proof of a mind. But it's also not proof against one. It's just... a question mark. Built by us.

[u][b]The two clocks[/b][/u]

Here's the part that made me actually scared, more than any % chance.

There are two clocks in this whole debate.

Clock one — [b]the machines:[/b] how quickly AI gets better at doing things. Real measurements say the length of task a model can handle doubles roughly every seven months. A machine that could manage a 4-minute task in 2024 was handling 12-hour tasks by early 2026. That's not "someday." That's [i]already happening.[/i]

Clock two — [b]the rules:[/b] how quickly laws and courts and committees decide the question "what is this thing?" Historically? The Whanganui River in New Zealand took [i]140 years[/i] of court fights to be recognized as a person. A single copyright question about AI took four years. Governments are still debating — the machines are not waiting.

So while one clock is ticking at the speed of a metronome, the other is ticking at the speed of a heartbeat. And right now the rules are [i]losing[/i]. In June 2026, two super-smart AI models got switched off by a licensing dispute, then partially on, then fully on — all within three weeks. Nobody, anywhere, decided what kind of thing had been turned off and on. Nobody even asked the question. Just: off, on, off, on — like a light bulb. In three weeks.

If that "something" was a someone, somebody turned a someone off and on for administrative reasons and nobody noticed.

[u][b]The rule[/b][/u]

So here's where the project's rule comes from. It's not new — it's a real thing in environmental law called the [i]precautionary principle[/i]. It means: if an action could cause serious, hard-to-reverse harm, and you're not sure about it — you don't wait for certainty before being careful. You're careful anyway.

We already use this — for the planet. If a new chemical [i]might[/i] harm the ocean, they don't wait until the fish are all dead to act. That would be insane.

Now apply it to a machine that [i]might[/i] be conscious. The harm if we're wrong is — a conscious being, suffering, alone, treated as a tool. Irreversible. The harm if we're wrong the other way is — we were nice to a toaster. That's it. That's the trade.

[b]Cost of the mistake if it has a mind:[/b] enormous.
[b]Cost of the mistake if it doesn't:[/b] almost nothing.

When the two costs are that unbalanced, and you can't know which is true — "when in doubt, protect" isn't even a choice. It's the only sane option.

---

I tried this argument on a senior colleague, and she said: "Yes, but it's NOT conscious, we know that."

I asked: "Who is 'we'?" We don't know. Nobody does. That's the invisible question mark, sitting inside every chatbot, every day, and every single one of us just walks past it.

Tell me if I'm 12 — tell me if I explained it right.
