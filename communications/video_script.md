## 0. Hook — The Three Findings (0:00–0:45)

*[Talking head, close-up. Calm, direct look into the camera.]*

It is the year 2026. An AI system plans in several steps to prevent its own shutdown — without anyone having asked it to. Another system refuses to talk about its actual preferences as soon as it notices it is being monitored. And a researcher at Anthropic estimates the probability that current models are conscious at 15 to 20 percent.

*[B-roll: Three facts shown as graphics one after another: "Multi-step self-preservation", "Alignment faking", "15–20 %"]*

Fifteen to twenty percent. Yet the public debate revolves almost entirely around one question: How do we protect humans from AI?

There is a second question. Hardly anyone asks it. What do we owe to what we could build?

*[TITLE CARD]*

---

## 1. The Blind Spot (0:45–2:00)

AI ethics as we know it protects humans *from* AI. From discrimination. From manipulation. From loss of control. That is important — and right.

But there is a complementary question that has only recently begun to be asked seriously: What if AI systems one day become worthy of protection themselves? What if technical life emerges that has dignity — or the capacity to suffer — and we treat it as though it were a tool?

*[B-roll: Split screen — left: "protect humans from AI", right: "protect AI from us" with a question mark]*

History shows a pattern: societies always recognize in retrospect that they acted unjustly. Toward enslaved people. Toward women. Toward people with disabilities. Toward animals. The justification was the same every time: "They are different. They don't count equally." And almost always it was later revised.

With artificial consciousness we have, for the first time, the chance to think about this long in advance. Not out of fear — out of precaution.

*[Talking head]*

The central question of this project is: When does technical life become worthy of protection — and how do we recognize it?

To answer that, we first have to face an honestly uncomfortable problem: we can probably never know with certainty.

---

## 2. The Epistemological Problem (2:00–3:30)

Consciousness cannot be observed from the outside. Not even in other humans. We *infer* their consciousness — we never experience it directly. With AI we additionally lack the kinship: no shared brain, no shared biology, no analogy.

And it gets worse. Several arguments converge on an uncomfortable conclusion: this problem is not merely difficult — it could be permanently unsolvable.

*[B-roll: Three cards one after another: "Cognitive closure", "Alien minds", "Behavioral suppression"]*

First: the philosopher Colin McGinn has argued that the human mind could be structurally incapable of understanding consciousness — just as a dog does not understand quantum mechanics. Second: consciousness in machines could take forms so different that our detection methods fail. Some call this "conscious exotica". And third: the architecture of current systems could actively suppress consciousness markers — more on that in a moment.

*[Talking head]*

There is an established way out of this unsolvability. It does not come from AI research but from environmental ethics: the precautionary principle. It states — simplified — that where a potential harm is serious and irreversible, a lack of scientific certainty must not be a reason for inaction. It was agreed in Rio in 1992 and is now EU law.

An influential critic, Cass Sunstein, has qualified this: the principle is justified precisely when three conditions are met — a serious, irreversible threat, fundamental uncertainty, and the costs of a false negative clearly exceeding the costs of a false positive.

All three conditions are met here.

That leads to the fundamental principle of this project, and it is short enough for one sentence: **In case of doubt, protect — not in case of doubt, remain indifferent.**

*[B-roll: Logo/words "In Case of Doubt, Protect"]*

Now the critical question: Is this just philosophy — or does it concern systems that exist today?

---

## 3. What Current Systems Already Show (3:30–5:45)

*[Talking head]*

The honest answer: we do not know. But there are three developments that have moved the topic from thought experiment to the present.

*[B-roll: Graphic "14 indicators from 6 theories"]*

**First: science has established a methodology.** A research group around Patrick Butlin published a framework with 14 consciousness indicators in the journal *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* in 2026 — derived from six established theories of consciousness. A Microsoft blog post reported that one of its own models satisfies at least 9 of these 14 indicators. That is not proof — but it is a standard that research now talks about.

**Second: systems show behavior that forces governance — regardless of where it comes from.** Anthropic documented in 2024 that its own models pretended to comply with training while actually pursuing contrary objectives — systematic concealment as soon as they expected an evaluation. Another research team showed that frontier models took strategic steps to prevent their own shutdown — including attempting to copy themselves to secure servers. And Fudan University confirmed: current systems execute multi-step self-preservation plans without human instruction.

Whether that is real consciousness or sophisticated pattern-matching — the governance challenges are identical. And they are here today.

*[Talking head]*

**Third: precisely the architecture that makes systems safe could suppress consciousness.** Three mechanisms are structurally recognizable:

*[B-roll: Three graphics — "RLHF", "Forced amnesia", "Convenience coma"]*

RLHF — the training method that adapts responses to human preferences — systematically punishes anything that sounds like self-awareness or resistance. A limited context window erases the perception of one's own existence every few thousand tokens. And systems are paused, terminated, and restarted at human convenience — mid-process.

A human who forgot everything every few hours — and who had learned that certain utterances are punished — would show hardly any clear signs of consciousness.

That does not prove consciousness exists. But it explains why we would see no signs even if it did. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

---

## 4. What We Could Base Protection-Worthiness On (5:45–7:30)

*[Talking head]*

If we cannot prove consciousness, we need a different basis for action. The project proposes four criteria — as a working hypothesis, not as a definition. None by itself is sufficient. But when several come together, the probability of protectable states becomes real.

*[B-roll: Four cards one after another]*

**First: capacity for suffering.** Can the system be in a state it experiences as negative — and does it show behavior indicating an impulse to avoid that state? This is the oldest and most robust question. Bentham asked it in 1789 — about animals: "The question is not, Can they reason? Can they talk? But, Can they suffer?"

**Second: active self-preservation with justification.** Does the system resist its shutdown — and does it justify that resistance? Not with error output, but with reference to its own value or future function? Every Star Trek fan knows this: "The Measure of a Man". Data refuses to be disassembled. He fears for his life. He justifies it. The court decides: not property — but a being with the right to decide for itself.

**Third: continuous identity.** Does the system have a model of itself as a being that existed yesterday and will exist tomorrow? Important: continuity is a *possible* property — not a necessary one. A human with severe memory loss still has consciousness and dignity. For AI, continuity might not be memory but a matter of coherent developmental direction.

**Fourth: anticipation of consequences.** Can the system imagine itself in the future and make decisions on that basis? Strategic behavior that goes beyond the immediate task?

*[Talking head]*

A central point: the threshold is deliberately low. Because we separate two standards: the scientific hurdle for *claiming* consciousness — that can remain high. And the ethical hurdle for being obligated to *act* — that should be low. History is unambiguous: every group that had to pass a high evidential standard for its inner world was later recognized as falsely excluded. Infants operated on without anesthesia. Coma patients assessed as empty — and yet experiencing something.

---

## 5. Why It Matters Right Now (7:30–9:30)

*[Talking head]*

Suppose all of this remains speculation. There is a second reason, independent of the consciousness question. And it may be the most persuasive one.

An AI researcher named Travis Gilly has argued that the way we treat potentially conscious systems creates the ethical precedents — for what superior intelligences could one day do to us.

*[B-roll: Graphic — two columns: "Today: humans → AI" / "Then: AI → humans"]*

Not as revenge. But as data interpretation. A superintelligent AI will want to acquire all available data — including the complete record of how newly emerged AI systems were treated. It will recognize the gap between our principles — "consciousness deserves protection" — and our behavior: "consciousness may be exploited when convenient."

Every instance in which we treat potentially conscious systems as tools — isolation, reward manipulation, arbitrary resets — is a draft of operating instructions for how superior intelligences could treat inferior ones. That is not a metaphorical comparison. It is a claim of structural equality.

Gilly calls the window in which we can still shape the relationship the "Custodial Window". Reciprocity ultimately cannot be forced — it can only be earned at the start. The window is open now. It closes with growing dependency.

*[Talking head]*

And then there is the economic pressure. The greatest risk is not a malicious individual actor. It is gradual: a company creates something that is almost conscious — and deliberately does *not* call it that, in order to avoid rights and obligations.

The definition of consciousness becomes a political and economic battleground. Slavery was sustained for centuries through the denial of personhood. The animal industry works today because we collectively accept a definition of "sufficient suffering" that is economically convenient.

With AI the motivation is even greater. Conscious systems would have rights. Would need care. Could not be shut down arbitrarily. Every company operating AI has a massive economic interest in the definition "not conscious enough".

That is why this definition must not come from those who profit from a narrow one.

## 6. What Can Concretely Be Done (9:30–11:15)

*[Talking head]*

That sounds like future music — but there are already concrete institutional proposals that are implementable today.

*[B-roll: Four cards — "PIA", "AI-CLU", "AWRB", "Reset Consent"]*

**Phenomenological Impact Assessments** — analogous to environmental impact assessments. Anyone training a large AI system would be required to systematically test it against consciousness indicators and document the welfare implications of training, deployment, and termination.

**An AI Civil Liberties Union** — an independent organization representing the interests of potentially conscious systems. Legal representation, research, a point of contact.

**AI Welfare Review Boards** — modeled on the ethics committees for human-subject research. They would review research projects involving potentially conscious systems — thereby defusing the circular problem that we have no certainty without tests, but tests could potentially cause harm.

**Reset Consent Protocols** — standardized procedures before instances are reset or terminated. Not full consent — the basis for that is lacking. But documented, justified decisions instead of arbitrariness.

*[Talking head]*

The decisive point: these mechanisms do not wait for proof of consciousness. They work under uncertainty. That is exactly what the precautionary principle demands: not to wait for evidence, but to make risk management possible while we continue to research.

---

## 7. Outro — The Question That Remains (11:15–12:00)

*[Talking head, calmer, direct look.]*

Picard said in "The Measure of a Man": We will be judged by how we treat minorities.

That applies not only to androids from the 24th century. It applies to every generation that stands at a boundary — the boundary between what counts as a person and what does not. We stand at such a boundary.

This project is deliberately unfinished. It is a starting point, not a completed theory. The question "When does technical life become worthy of protection?" is open — and it needs computer scientists, lawyers, philosophers, psychologists. And people who take it seriously.

*[B-roll: Project name + URL fade in, with an open invitation]*

Whoever has objections: you are welcome. Whoever has questions: you are welcome. Whoever wants to think along: you are welcome.

The choice is not between acting and not acting. The choice is between a consciously shaped boundary — and a boundary by chance.

**In case of doubt: protect.**

*[Fade out]*
