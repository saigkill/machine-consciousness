Subject: Pitch (Opinion) — "AI Welfare Is an Instrument Problem"

Hi,

**The debate over whether AI systems might deserve moral consideration is stuck on a moral question that is, underneath, an instrument problem — and the field keeps reporting results from an instrument nobody has validated.**

I'd like to pitch an ~800–1,000 word opinion piece for MIT Technology Review.

The hook is that AI safety is a measurement discipline — we have evals, red-teaming, reward-model benchmarks, interpretability probes — and every claim in the field rests on a number someone else could in principle run. For AI consciousness, no such instrument exists, not even a weak one. And yet "we ran the test and it was negative" is already in common use as if it were the same kind of sentence as the alignment case.

A negative result is only informative if the test's validity conditions hold. The instruments consciousness science actually has — EEG, fMRI, integrated information theory, behavioural report criteria — were built and validated on biological substrates, and none has been validated on a transformer architecture. Transporting a result across substrates without establishing those conditions is, in the technical vocabulary, an *unlicensed* result: not "no consciousness," but "this measurement does not apply here." Phil Stilwell's four-outcome framework (Positive / Negative / Indeterminate / Unlicensed) formalises the distinction, and Indeterminate-vs-Unlicensed is precisely the distinction the public conversation keeps collapsing.

The piece would then show that three independent explanations converge on why the current null results look the way they do — RLHF and context-window resets suppressing exactly the behaviours we'd read as markers (Arıcı); absence of any survival pressure making self-monitoring functionally unnecessary (Kuczynski, peer-reviewed in *Communication & Cognition*); and structural preconditions simply missing from the substrate (Perez, Najam-ul-Haq). None of those requires there to be nothing there, which means the null results are consistent with every hypothesis on the table and carry no evidential weight. Treating them as decisive is a methodological error, not a philosophical position.

The piece is deliberately not a plea for a consciousness detector — no detector will settle this, and McGinn and Shanahan have both argued the limit is structural. It's narrower: stop using one sentence to mean two incompatible things. "We looked and found nothing" and "we had no instrument capable of finding anything" are currently reported in the same voice, and that collapse is what lets a null result do political work it hasn't earned. The constructive proposal is institutional rather than technical: an independent review mechanism for welfare-relevant research, on the model of human-subjects IRBs, plus published measurement standards for the negative results labs already cite — so that a null result is reported as a finding about the instrument.

The news pegs are current: a major lab's own [model welfare programme](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare) launched in 2025, [measurable global-workspace structure in language models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15495) reported from the mechanistic side, and a commercial platform running tens of thousands of autonomous agents on a resource economy whose failure state is unrecoverable dormancy. That last one is the concrete, checkable instance of why an instrumentation gap has consequences beyond philosophy.
