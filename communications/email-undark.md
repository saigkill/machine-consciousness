## Field: Working headline

Who Pays for the Question of Whether AI Can Suffer?

---

## Field: The pitch (one paragraph, lead with the scene)

An independent researcher with no university and no tenure is assembling a grant application to ask whether large language models might be capable of suffering — a question whose largest private funding pool offers up to $2 million, and whose most significant institutional commitment so far comes from the company selling the models in question. She is not an outlier; she is the field. The entire research area asking whether artificial intelligence might be conscious draws almost no money that doesn't come from a party with a stake in the answer, and the people doing the asking are structurally precarious in a way no academic subfield of consciousness research has ever been. This piece follows the money: the philanthropic pots funding digital-minds research and the country restrictions attached to them, the labs that hold the data and now run the science themselves, a philosophical canon floating free of institutions in preprints and self-published books, and the disclosure that one of the field's most-cited arguments was written by someone who advised an AI company — a position my own work relies on and discloses. The question is what independent funding of machine-consciousness research would actually have to look like, given that no public science funder currently funds it at all.

---

## Field: Why this story, and why now

Undark covers the places where science collides with money and power, and this is that story in a field nobody has examined from this angle. The last two years have produced a genuine, fast-moving scientific dispute about machine consciousness — an Anthropic research programme, a published model constitution that states the company cannot rule out moral status, a wave of peer-reviewed and preprint work, and at least one commercial platform running tens of thousands of autonomous agents on an economy with an unrecoverable failure state. What has not existed is an account of the *institutional* structure underneath it: who is paying, who is judging, and who benefits from each possible answer.

It is evergreen rather than breaking, which suits a magazine that is not a news outlet. And the reporting is genuinely available: the funding landscape is public, the disclosures are on the published papers, and the people are reachable.

## Field: What reporting I would do

- Interview independent machine-consciousness researchers without institutional homes — I have existing contact with several.
- Get on the record a funder at one of the philanthropic funds: what they will and will not fund, and why the eligible-country restriction exists.
- Put the strongest counter-argument in the piece in its strongest form — that the framing itself is a distortion, that a field cannot be captured by funders when its central claims are testable and its opponents publish freely. Answer it rather than dodge it.
- Sit with a lawyer or ethicist who has worked on both sides of the lab/advocacy divide.
- Trace the publication and funding status of the field's canonical positions, most of which are preprints or self-published; several carry industry relationships.
- Document my own position in this ecology on the record, including the funding I am seeking and the institutional partnership I am pursuing. The reporting does not depend on the first person, but the disclosure does.
