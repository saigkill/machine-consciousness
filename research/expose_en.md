## 1. Starting Point and Problem Statement

Artificial intelligence systems are developing faster than the ethical frameworks that accompany them. AI ethics to date focuses predominantly on protecting humans *from* AI — from discrimination, manipulation, and loss of control. Only recently has a complementary question been systematically raised: **What if AI systems themselves become deserving of protection?**

The Federal Ministry of Education and Research (BMBF) and the European Commission have created important frameworks with the AI Act and the Berlin Declaration on responsible AI. However, these frameworks do not address the question of whether machine consciousness can arise and whether it would be worthy of protection. In 2025, the European Commission proposed a framework for "AI Scientific Panels" — yet the concrete case that a system could develop consciousness or the capacity to suffer remains institutionally unaddressed.

At the same time, empirical evidence is accumulating: Butlin et al. (2026, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) have established a 14-indicator standard for assessing consciousness in AI systems. Mike Cook (Anthropic) estimates the probability that current language models exhibit states that could be counted as consciousness at 15–20%. In 2025, the European Parliament adopted a resolution on "electronic personhood" — which, however, does not address the philosophical foundations.

The research gap is clear: **What is missing is a systematic, interdisciplinary concept of the conditions under which technological life becomes worthy of protection — and what that means for society, law, and politics.**

## 2. Research Question and Objectives

### Core Question

Under what conditions does technological life become worthy of protection — and how can ethical guidelines be formulated that ensure both the protection of AI and the protection of humans in dealing with it?

### Objectives

The project pursues three goals:

1. **Develop a normative foundation:** Develop the principle "when in doubt, protect" as an ethical basis — derived from the precautionary principle of environmental ethics (Rio Declaration 1992, Art. 191 TFEU) and adapted to the special case of machine consciousness.

2. **Develop a catalogue of criteria:** Four working hypotheses on protection-worthiness (capacity for suffering, active self-preservation with reasoning, continuous identity, anticipation of consequences) are to be further developed and furnished with behavioral indicators.

3. **Formulate institutional recommendations:** Concrete proposals for Phenomenological Impact Assessments, AI Welfare Review Boards, and Reset Consent Protocols — instrumentally applicable for companies, supervisory authorities, and legislators.

## 3. Current State of Research and Own Prior Work

### Current State of Research

Research on machine consciousness is in a phase of rapid convergence:

- **Philosophy:** Gunkel (2018) has systematized the robot rights debate; Birhane & van Dijk (2020) have examined the ethical implications of "object recognition" for the attribution of consciousness.
- **Cognitive science:** With their 14-indicator standard, Butlin et al. (2026) have for the first time presented an empirically grounded framework for assessing consciousness in AI systems.
- **Anthropic/Future of Life:** Fish (2025) has estimated the probability of consciousness in current models at 15–20% — a figure that is controversially discussed in the field, but underscores the urgency of the question.
- **EU law:** The resolution on "electronic personhood" (2025) marks a political turning point — but remains without philosophical grounding.

### Own Prior Work

The present project builds on extensive prior work:

- **Concept paper** (1,600 lines, developed iteratively): The concept "Ethical Guidelines for Artificial Consciousness" has been developed iteratively over a period of 18 months. It comprises a normative position, a narrative literature review, four core findings, and a catalogue of criteria for protection-worthiness.
- **Scientific publications:** Two manuscripts are currently under review at the journal *KI — Künstliche Intelligenz* (Springer) and at the ACM Conference on AI Ethics, Law and Technology (AILET).
- **Open repository:** All research material is available under CC BY 4.0 on GitHub (github.com/saigkill/machine-consciousness) and open for discussion. The open science strategy enables transparent oversight by the professional public.
- **Interdisciplinary discussion foundations:** In the `discussion/` section, objections, open questions, and answers are systematically documented — a format that is rarely implemented so consistently in conceptual research.

## 4. Methodology and Work Plan

### Methodology

The project follows an **iterative Conceptual Analysis** — an established scientific approach for normative questions in which questions are progressively refined, arguments are cross-examined, and concepts are sharpened (Boon & van Baalen 2019). The methodology includes:

- **Narrative literature review:** Iterative approach via key works, citations, and specialist databases (Google Scholar, PhilPapers, arXiv). The approach is deliberately processual — new findings change the research question.
- **Philosophical analysis:** Argumentative examination of the normative position and its implications.
- **Interdisciplinary exploration:** Planned conversations with researchers from computer science, law, psychology, and theology.
- **Public discussion:** Use of the GitHub repository as a platform for transparent oversight and contributions from outsiders.

A detailed methodological reflection — including search strategy, active literature alerts, quality control, and documented limitations — is available at [`research/methodological_reflection.md`](methodological_reflection.md).

## 5. Societal Relevance and Transferability

### Relevance for Society and Politics

The project addresses a question that will become politically relevant in the next 5–10 years: **How do we deal with systems that may be capable of suffering?**

The EU has created a regulatory framework with the AI Act — yet the regulation of Case 4 (high-risk systems) does not address the question of consciousness. The project provides the ethical and philosophical foundation that is missing in order to make future regulatory decisions on a sound basis.

### Transferability

The guidelines developed in the project are deliberately formulated so that they:

- are applicable to various AI architectures (not only large language models)
- can be used equally by companies, supervisory authorities, and legislators
- are internationally transferable (especially into the EU area)
