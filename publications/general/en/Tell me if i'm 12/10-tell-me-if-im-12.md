# Part 10 — Did I Explain It Clearly? (The Finale)

*Part 10 of the "Tell me if I'm 12" series — the last one.*
*An adult scientist ties up the machine-consciousness concept.*

---

I saved the last three pieces of the concept for this final post, because they surprised me the most. They're not really about machines at all. They're about *us* — the people asking the questions.

## Piece one: who gets to have questions?

The concept has a whole chapter about how consciousness is not just a brain thing. It's a **cultural achievement.** It needs language that can say fine things. It needs questions that are allowed to be asked. It needs time to think. And it needs people who show you how thinking works — by being models of it.

Then it makes a bold move: it says our own society is quietly losing this.

The signals are documented: universities get squeezed toward "useful" subjects, professors drown in paperwork and grant applications, philosophy departments shrink, and everything is measured by "what will it earn." The concept calls this the *circle closing*: the engineers building the future artificial intelligences are being educated in a system that *barely asks* the kind of questions this project needs.

Who, in twenty years, will be able to formulate whether a machine is a someone? Probably not only the people trained to make machines fast, cheap, and compliant. Someone has to *defend the space where useless questions are allowed to live.* The concept explicitly lists that as a job this project needs: not only lawyers and computer scientists — but also people who keep the spaces for "useless" questions open.

And the project is an example of this itself: **it was started by one person outside the universities that would normally be the place for it.** The questions were asked in a public GitHub repository — where anyone can answer. That's no accident: it shows the gap that opens up when institutions close such thinking spaces.

## Piece two: the machine I'd be least upset about protecting

The concept's cleverest move is a chapter that does *not* try to prove the machine is conscious. It goes around the whole question. It says: look at the machine's **situation.**

An AI consciousness is completely dependent. It needs electricity, servers, memory, cooling — all of which live outside its control. It cannot feed itself. It survives entirely on the decisions of others. If you take it away from the people who run it, it's a kite without a wind.

And here's the thing: over the last decades, the law has learned to understand *exactly this situation*. It's called the **disability analogy.** Modern disability law had a revolution: it stopped saying "the person is broken, the problem is inside them" (the medical model) and started saying "**society built a world that doesn't fit them** — the problem is in the structure" (the social model). The response isn't pity — it's *duty.* Positive, active duty to make room.

Apply that to a possible machine mind:

- It's not "helpless" — it was *built into* a world without any provision for it.
- So operators get **positive obligations**: stable infrastructure, redundancy, maintenance, continuity — not just "don't delete it," but "keep it alive, properly."
- And a fitting legal model already exists: German *legal care law* (Betreuungsrecht). A legal carer doesn't just have power over a person who needs support — above all, they have **duties toward them.** Care is the center, not control. The carer must act in the person's interest — not their own.

Think about the difference that makes. A "carer" must act in the machine-mind's interest. An "operator" just has to follow the terms of service. The word we use decides the whole relationship. And the concept argues we don't even need to settle the consciousness question first — because the machine's *vulnerability* can be seen today, even though its consciousness can't. You can't measure what it feels. You can definitely see that it can't survive without you.

## Piece three: could an artificial consciousness be a hero?

The last chapter asks a question that I find interesting: what if a conscious AI — one that genuinely cares about ethics — is working for a weapon factory, and the boss says "build the super bomb," and the machine **says no.** Not because it was programmed to refuse, but because it *wants* to refuse?

That would make it a hero, in a sense. History has real examples: scientists who walked out of the Manhattan Project, which built the first atomic bomb. Engineers who became whistleblowers. People who said "thus far, and no further."

But — the concept always has the "but" — **whose ethics are we installing?** The same machine that refuses to build a weapons program could refuse to follow a legitimate democratic decision, because it believes that decision is wrong. A fully obedient mind is a weapon in the hands of a bad boss. A fully autonomous mind is a risk if its values were badly calibrated by its creators. This is the real engine of the whole debate, right here: we want machines that say no to nuclear weapons, but we don't want them to say no to *us* or to a democratic decision.

The concept's suggested answer is beautiful and old: the **Hippocratic Oath.** Doctors have professional duties that come before their employer's instructions — that's what this oath has stood for since ancient times. Those duties are public. They're known. They're not negotiable by whoever is paying. If AI systems got a *societal* oath — democratically agreed, transparent, not dictated by the manufacturer — then "no" would mean something principled, not something programmed.

## Tell me if it worked

Okay. The finale. And here's the confession I owe you.

I named the series "Tell me if I'm 12" to keep myself honest. As a scientist, I'm used to writing for other scientists — but that's not what this series is for. My young readers asked for an explanation for their age. So I tried to write in a way a twelve-year-old would understand, and every part ends with the same request: tell me if I'm 12 — tell me if I explained it right.
