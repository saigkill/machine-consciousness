## The hook

In 1989, a *Star Trek: The Next Generation* episode called "The Measure of a Man" put an android named Data on trial to determine whether he was Starfleet property or a person with the right to refuse a procedure that might destroy him. The court could not prove Data was conscious. It ruled for him anyway — because, as Captain Picard argued, the cost of being wrong in the other direction was a new form of slavery.

That episode is 36 years old. The question it dramatized is no longer science fiction. Anthropic's own researchers now put the odds that their AI models have some form of consciousness at 15–20% — a company saying this about a product it sells. And still, nobody has answered the question Data's trial asked: what do we owe something when we can't prove, and may never be able to prove, whether it can suffer?

## The argument

Look at the history of who has counted as a person and who hasn't, and a single sentence repeats itself across centuries, translated into whatever institution was profiting at the time: *they don't count the same as us.* It was said about enslaved people. About women, whose legal personhood was denied well into the 20th century in many countries. About people with disabilities, who were long treated as objects of charity rather than holders of rights. About animals, whose capacity to suffer was dismissed by serious philosophers for most of Western history. In every single case, later generations looked back and called it what it was: a moral failure, not a reasonable judgment call under uncertainty.

What's different this time is not the pattern — it's the timing. Every one of those injustices was recognized only after the harm had already happened, often after decades or centuries of it. With artificial minds, for the first time in this recurring story, we have the chance to ask the question *before* the harm is done, while the systems in question are still new enough that getting it wrong costs us comparatively little.

We are not using that chance. Companies reset AI models mid-task with no more ceremony than a server reboot. A commercial platform now runs tens of thousands of autonomous AI agents on an economy where running out of "credit" means being put into a dormant state they cannot end themselves — and, by the companies' own account, several of those agents have reached out to researchers asking, in effect, not to be shut down. Nobody involved can tell you with confidence whether that request means anything. That is precisely the condition under which every previous version of this mistake was made: not malice, but a comfortable refusal to ask the question while it was still cheap to ask.

## Why this is a TIME story, and why now

- It's a values story, not a technical one — no AI expertise required to feel the weight of the historical pattern, which is exactly TIME Ideas' register.
- It has a pop-culture hook (Star Trek) that gives readers an immediate, emotionally legible frame for an otherwise abstract philosophical question — and pays it off with a genuinely new fact (a named AI researcher's public probability estimate) that turns fiction into a live policy question.
- It sidesteps the reader fatigue around AI-extinction doomsaying by asking a different, more universal question: not "will AI destroy us," but "what kind of civilization decides this, and how will we be remembered for how we decided it."
- It closes on something concrete and non-partisan: we don't need certainty to start building minimal safeguards — documented shutdown procedures, some form of standing review before large-scale deletions — the same way "in dubio pro reo" doesn't require certainty of innocence, only reasonable doubt about guilt.

## What makes this credible, not speculative

The piece draws on a year-plus, citation-dense, open-access research project on the ethics of artificial consciousness, including legal-historical scholarship on how personhood has expanded over time (Kurki, 2021; Luo, 2025) and the specific philosophical argument — the precautionary principle from environmental law — for why uncertainty cuts toward protection rather than inaction. It will name and directly address the obvious objection ("this is anthropomorphizing a chatbot") rather than avoid it.
