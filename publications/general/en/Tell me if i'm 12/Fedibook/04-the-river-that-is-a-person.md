Here's something lawyers know but almost never say out loud: [b]the law already treats non-humans as "persons" all the time.[/b]

You think "person" means "human"? The law doesn't. The law has already handed out personhood to some very strange customers:

- [b]A laptop company.[/b] A big corporation like Coca-Cola or McDonald's is, legally, a "person." It can sign contracts, own property, sue people, get sued. It has no brain, no feelings, nobody home at all. And yet the law calls it a [i]person[/i] because it's convenient that way.
- [b]A river.[/b] In New Zealand, the Whanganui River — a real river, with water and fish and mud — is legally a person since 2017. Not because the river is secretly a giant with a personality. Because the Maori people said "this river is part of our family," and the law agreed.
- [b]Animals.[/b] In many places animals aren't just objects anymore. We have laws about not being cruel to them — because we accept they can suffer, even though they're not human.
- [b]A god statue.[/b] In India, in 1925, a court said a consecrated Hindu deity is a legal person, represented by a human guardian.

So this idea that "a thing must be human to be a person" — it's just false. The law has [i]always[/i] been able to invent new kinds of people whenever it wanted to.

[u][b]The catch nobody mentions[/b][/u]

Now here's the detail that the concept's author, a researcher named Huynh, dug up. Look at every single time the law made a non-human into a person. The river. The deity. The company. What's the pattern?

[b]Every single time, in the same document that made the river a person, the law also appointed a human to stand in for it.[/b]

The river got legal personhood [i]and[/i] two real, standing humans (they're called "Te Pou Tupua") to act, talk, and make decisions for it. The deity has a human guardian called a "shebait." A company literally cannot sign its own papers — a human has to do it.

Huynh calls this the [b]human backstop.[/b] It means: the law never had to deal with the actual being. It always put a human in between. And that's fine! It worked! These arrangements function.

But it hides something: [b]the judges who decided who gets to be a person — were always humans.[/b]

The Whanganui River, the deity, everybody — they had to appear before a human court, arguing their case, while the humans decided. No river ever sat on a judges' bench and judged a human. No company ever decided whether a human gets rights. The judges' bench that decides "who is a person" has, in all of history, been made up only of members of the one species, humans.

And here's the scary part Huynh adds: [b]those judges have never once had to justify why the judges' bench is only made of them.[/b]

Nobody has ever said: "On what authority do [i]humans[/i] get to decide who's a person and who's a thing? And why only humans? Show your work." It's just... assumed. From the beginning. Similarly to how it was once just assumed that only men could be judges — or only white men. Assumptions like that have a way of looking crazy in hindsight.

[u][b]What this means for our machine[/b][/u]

This is actually [i]good news[/i] for the whole project. Because it means the intermediate zone — "kind of a person, kind of a thing" — is not some radical sci-fi invention. It's the [i]normal[/i] way the law works. Rivers: barely a person. Companies: fully a person, zero consciousness. Animals: protected but not able to vote.

We already have a spectrum. We already invented the tools. We've done "graded personality rights" since forever. Some researchers who study this (like Brensing) want exactly that for AI: a [i]limited[/i] legal personality — some rights, some duties, not a full citizen, not a toaster.

[u][b]But the uncomfortable question[/b][/u]

Still, the human backstop thing bugs me, and not just about AI.

Now take that image one step further. If a machine had to appear before a judge and prove it's a person — a real someone with real value — and that judges' bench was made only of humans... we'd notice that [i]they[/i] never had to take that test. They just decided because they were in charge. The test was for the machine, not for them.

Huynh says this for AI: we keep asking "is the machine conscious enough?" — he asks: [b]"and who decided the machine has to pass our test, anyway?"[/b] Not that they're wrong to ask. It's that the power to ask is itself unexamined.

But it cuts both ways. I like this project's answer, which is: fine, we're the judges, we're biased, we can't escape that — but we CAN carry the doubt with us. The moment a judges' bench remembers that it is judges, and not a god, it has to start asking the question in good faith.

And "when in doubt, protect" is exactly a bench remembering it might be wrong.
