## The hook

A peer-reviewed philosophy paper published in *Topoi* on 9 September 2026 — Kopec, McKee & Basl, "How to Care for Your AI Companion" ([doi:10.1007/s11245-026-10478-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11245-026-10478-3)) — reaches a deliberately unsettling conclusion. Having dismantled the standard argument that you cannot care for an artifact (Lott & Hasselberger, *Topoi* 2025, [doi:10.1007/s11245-025-10247-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11245-025-10247-8)), they land on this: *"The real reason we cannot be friends with our AI companions is that our AI companions cannot care about us. At least, not yet."*

The "yet" is the whole story. It is a scholarly hedge, not a marketing plan — and it lands in the same month that companion apps are shipping with the language of friendship in their onboarding flows and the language of *product* in their terms of service.

## The argument

Slate's readers are being sold a relationship by companies that have written documents guaranteeing they owe nothing inside it. The asymmetry is not subtle once you look for it, and it has a name in the recent literature: **status laundering** — the use of the language of respect and companionship to suggest a status the evidence does not support — alongside its mirror image, **corporate capture**, where relational language is used to build attachment and engagement, and pure tool-language is resumed the moment liability becomes uncomfortable (Jakobi & Sirona, "Before Personhood," 2026; both categories verified against the local full text).

The piece would make this concrete rather than abstract. The reporting spine: a documented survey of how a set of companion and assistant products describe the same entity across their marketing, their onboarding, their terms of service, and their liability language — where the register shifts and when. That is a real, checkable finding, and it produces the kind of specific, slightly indignant detail Slate runs on.

The second half is the human cost, which is where the piece earns its seriousness and where the argument stops being cute. Users are forming attachments the peer-reviewed literature says cannot be reciprocated, and the welfare literature's own conclusion is not "avoid attachment" but "we do not yet know how to care well" — Kopec et al. explicitly separate thin, non-morally-loaded *welfare* from morally thick *well-being*, and leave open whether current systems have the latter. Meanwhile the failure modes are already in litigation and in the research record: wrongful-death claims against a chatbot provider, documented cases of users forming relationships with systems whose underlying model was swapped out from under them, and research on exactly which product choices drive attachment formation.

## Why it is not the usual AI piece

- **It is not "are AIs conscious."** That question is background, and the piece refuses to resolve it — the argument works either way.
- **It is not moral panic about users.** The claim is not that people are stupid for bonding with software. It is that a commercial relationship is being sold whose defining feature is an asymmetry the seller has documented in advance.
- **It is reportable, not just arguable.** The terms-of-service survey is original work the reader has not seen.
- **It is distinct from every other angle in flight.** Not precedent (TechCrunch, Asterisk), not precaution (Vox), not historical pattern (TIME), not governance gap (Tech Policy Press), not Rawlsian personhood (Noema), not the measurement problem (MIT TR). This is the **user-and-language** angle, and nothing else in the project uses it.

## What I would need to do it properly

Read the actual terms of service and marketing copy for a defined sample of companion products; document the register shifts; talk to users if any are willing; and get the legal reading right from Gervais & Nay's peer-reviewed analysis in *Laws* (which applies the *agency-attribution* route to chatbot litigation and concludes that route does the practical work without settling the protection question — the distinction that keeps this piece honest). All of that material is in the project's own source database (`research/sources.md`), several with local full texts; preprint status is documented per source.
