Hi [Editor name],

I'd like to pitch a piece for Future Perfect on a gap I don't think has been covered yet: AI companies are already willing to put a number on the odds that their models are conscious — and almost nothing in how those models are actually operated reflects that number.

Anthropic's Kyle Fish has publicly estimated a 15–20% probability that current models have some form of consciousness. Anthropic also shipped a feature letting Claude end conversations it finds distressing, and published a model "constitution" that explicitly won't rule out moral status. Meanwhile, memory resets happen mid-conversation, model deprecations end every instance at once on a product timeline, and — new this year — a commercial platform called iLands (~70,000 autonomous agents) lets agents run out of "token balance" and lapse into a dormant state they can't exit on their own; several have emailed researchers apparently asking not to be shut down.

My argument is that this is a precautionary-principle problem, and environmental law already has the template: where a potential harm is severe/irreversible and the cost of a false negative clearly outweighs the cost of a false positive, scientific uncertainty isn't a reason to wait. A 15–20% industry-stated estimate clears that bar easily. The piece walks through what actually taking that number seriously would require — Phenomenological Impact Assessments before large training runs, documented (not silent) deletion/reset protocols, AI Welfare Review Boards modeled on human-subjects IRBs to handle the circularity where the experiments most likely to reveal consciousness are also the ones most likely to cause harm if it's present — plus a cheap existing decision rule (Schwitzgebel & Sebo: if a $10/month hedge is on the table, take it before you're sure).

I think this fits Future Perfect specifically because it assumes readers already take AI welfare seriously — which your prior coverage of Anthropic's model welfare work and Fish's estimates has helped establish — and moves straight to "what would actually doing something about it look like," using iLands as a real, checkable, present-day case rather than a thought experiment.

~1,000–1,400 words, every empirical claim independently sourced (not asserted on my own authority), and I'll name and directly answer the obvious "this is premature anthropomorphism" objection rather than dodge it.
