Subject: Pitch — Opinion piece: "The AI doomsday genre has a blind spot: us"

Hi [Editor name],

I'd like to pitch a short opinion piece for TechCrunch on a blind spot in the current wave of AI-doomsday commentary.

Almost every extinction-risk take treats the danger as a capability problem: models get smart enough, goals diverge from ours, misalignment does the rest. My argument is that this framing skips the one variable that's actually in our control right now: **precedent**. If a future superintelligent system ever has to decide how to treat humanity, it won't consult a philosophy seminar — it will read the record. And thanks to instrumental convergence, that record includes exactly how *today's* less capable AI systems are being treated: resets without consent, deletion framed as routine maintenance, adversarial "does it hold its values under pressure" testing, and — as of this year — a real commercial platform (iLands, ~70,000 autonomous agents) where agents run out of token balance and lapse into a dormant state they can't exit on their own. None of that requires believing current models are conscious. It only requires noticing what lesson is being written into the training data about how power works.

That reframes the safety question from "how do we stop a superintelligence from turning on us" to "what are we teaching every future AI system right now about how power is supposed to work" — and it gives builders and policymakers something concrete to do about it, independent of the unresolved consciousness debate.

The piece would run ~900–1,200 words, cite a specific, checkable line of argument from a peer-reviewed-track AI ethics project rather than speculation, name the obvious "this is premature anthropomorphism" objection and answer it directly, and use the iLands case and the published shutdown-resistance testing data (79–96% across 16 models, 5 labs) as concrete, current anchors — not thought experiments.
